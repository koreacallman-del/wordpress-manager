## 1. Definition and history

### 1.1 What kimchi is
- **Fact:** Kimchi is a Korean fermented vegetable dish. Salted vegetables are seasoned and fermented, driven mainly by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) from the raw ingredients. The 2014 review by Park et al. frames kimchi as a "vegetable probiotic food" comparable to yogurt as a dairy probiotic food. It says LAB-driven fermentation suppresses putrefactive bacteria and can reduce pathogens.
- **Source:** Park KY et al. "Health benefits of kimchi (Korean fermented vegetables) as a probiotic food." *Journal of Medicinal Food* 17(1):6-20, 2014 (PMID 24456350). Abstract seen via third-party listing (nutrition-evidence.com/article/203117), not the journal site. Full author list [확인 필요].
- **Also:** A review found that fermentation is started by LAB originating from the raw ingredients, with garlic the most important source. See 2.1.
- **Level:** A (abstract only) | **Volatility:** low
- **Suggested wording:** "Kimchi is a Korean dish of salted, seasoned vegetables fermented mainly by lactic acid bacteria."

### 1.2 Early history (Three Kingdoms to Goryeo)
- **Fact:** The common account is that Three Kingdoms-era salt-pickled vegetables (turnips, eggplants, gourds and others) are the ancestor of kimchi. The Korean Food Promotion Institute (hansik.or.kr) describes it this way. The same source says dongchimi (a brine-based radish kimchi) appeared in the Goryeo period.
- **Source:** Korean Food Promotion Institute magazine, https://www.hansik.or.kr/magazines/list/magazineDetail/28/2975 (only the search snippet was seen; full article not read).
- **Fact:** The poet Yi Gyubo (1168-1241) wrote a 13th-century poem showing radish pickles/kimchi were common in Goryeo. Early forms were not chili-based.
- **Source:** Wikipedia "Kimchi" and hansik.or.kr, both as surfaced in search. Underlying primary source (Yi Gyubo's collected works) [확인 필요].
- **Fact (weak):** One popular account says the *Samguk Sagi* (record of the Three Kingdoms, covering 57 BC-668 AD) mentions onggi jars used to ferment vegetables.
- **Level:** A for the broad arc, B/C for the Samguk Sagi detail. **Recommendation:** avoid asserting that the Samguk Sagi mentions it unless a primary or scholarly source is found [확인 필요]. Safer wording: "salt-pickled vegetables go back to at least the Three Kingdoms era, according to Korean food-history sources."
- **Volatility:** low

### 1.3 Goryeo and early Joseon: no chili yet
- **Fact:** In Goryeo, kimchi developed into several types (pickles, dongchimi and nabak kimchi). Scallions and garlic were added. Chili powder and jeotgal (salted seafood) were not yet used.
- **Fact:** The cookbook *Eumsik dimibang* (c. 1670) lists salt, soybean paste and wheat bran as pickling ingredients but not chili.
- **Source:** Wikipedia "Kimchi" as surfaced in search; underlying cookbook date [확인 필요].
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low

### 1.4 Chili pepper introduction (16th-17th century) and when it became standard
- **Fact:** Chili peppers are native to the Americas. They reached East Asia through Portuguese traders.
- **Fact:** Historians place their arrival in Korea around the Imjin War (1592). The first Korean written mention is in *Jibong yuseol*, an encyclopedia published in 1614. A later farm-management text, *Sallim gyeongje*, describes kimchi made with chili.
- **Fact:** Chili use in kimchi became widespread only in the 19th century, per Wikipedia. The hansik.or.kr summary also says gochugaru and jeotgal came into use from the early 19th century. One explanation offered is that chili masked the fishy smell of jeotgal.
- **Source:** Wikipedia "Kimchi" (surfaced via search); hansik.or.kr magazine 28/2975.
- **Conflict (important):** Sources disagree on timing. One says Chinese cabbage, red pepper and salted seafood became the main ingredients in the 17th century. Another says Chinese (napa) cabbage reached Korea only at the end of the 19th century, with whole-cabbage kimchi in a cookbook of that period. Popular sites say chili became a staple by the 18th century.
- **Recommendation:** Write "chili arrived in Korea in the late 16th to early 17th century, but spread into standard kimchi recipes only gradually, becoming widespread by the 19th century." Avoid a single hard date for "red napa kimchi." Napa cabbage kimchi timing [확인 필요].
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low (scholarly dating can shift)
- **Link:** the writer may bridge to "Korean Fermented Foods Explained" (ID 3423).

### 1.5 UNESCO status
- **Fact:** "Kimjang, making and sharing kimchi in the Republic of Korea" was inscribed in 2013 (8.COM) on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The committee met in Baku, Azerbaijan, 2-7 December 2013.
- **Fact:** The UNESCO description says kimjang is a yearly cycle, with late autumn the peak season when communities make and share large quantities of kimchi so every household has enough for winter. Methods and ingredients are passed on within households, often from mother-in-law to daughter-in-law.
- **Source:** UNESCO https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00881 (listing confirmed via search; page text not opened). Also https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/silk-road-themes/intangible-cultural-heritage/kimjang-making-and-sharing-kimchi-republic-korea
- **Note:** The inscription covers *kimjang* (the communal making and sharing), not kimchi the food itself. Be precise.
- **Statistics conflict:** Korean Cultural Heritage Administration surveys are reported variously as "90% of Koreans still practice it" (a 2012 survey) and "80% make or take part" (a 2011 survey, AsiaNews). **Do not use either figure** unless the survey is located [확인 필요].
- **Level:** A | **Volatility:** low
- **Unverified:** "Kimchi Day, Nov 22" appeared in a search title (Asia News Network) only [확인 필요].

## 2. Fermentation science

### 2.1 Microbial succession
- **Fact:** Heterofermentative LAB of the genera *Leuconostoc*, *Lactobacillus* (now split into several genera) and *Weissella* are the key players. Review: "Kimchi microflora: history, current status, and perspectives for industrial kimchi production," *Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology* (indexed via AGRIS and CAU repository). Year/authors [확인 필요]; I believe 2014 but this was not confirmed.
- **Fact:** *Leuconostoc* and *Weissella* dominate early. A 500-day closed-ecosystem study found *Latilactobacillus* and *Levilactobacillus* dominant in the middle and late stages, respectively. Source: "Long-term population dynamics of viable microbes in a closed ecosystem of fermented vegetables" (Korea University repository; journal/year [확인 필요]).
- **Fact:** A 2006 study ("Microbial population dynamics of kimchi, a fermented cabbage product," PMID 16553862) found *Leuconostoc citreum* and *L. gasicomitatum* predominated in the first growth phase, and *Weissella koreensis* in the second. Authors/journal [확인 필요].
- **Fact:** A Korean review notes *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* is the major organism during the optimal ripening period at low temperature. Another study reports *Lactobacillus sakei* in the initial stage and *Leu. mesenteroides* at optimum ripening.
- **Important nuance for the article:** The classic textbook line "Leuconostoc early, Lactobacillus late" is a good simplification, but the real species vary by recipe, temperature, salt and batch. Studies show *Weissella*, *Leuconostoc citreum* and *Lactobacillus sakei* in the early or middle phase. Suggest "typically" and "in many studies."
- **Fact (garlic as inoculum):** Studies found *Leuconostoc* and *Weissella* were mainly derived from garlic. Ginger and leek were secondary sources. Napa cabbage itself had low LAB numbers. Source: "Garlic is a source of major lactic acid bacteria for early-stage fermentation of cabbage-kimchi" (Chung-Ang University repository; authors/year [확인 필요]).
- **Fact (LAB counts):** LAB in kimchi reach about 9-10 log CFU/g (roughly 10^9-10^10), per a description of the Cha et al. 2024 review (see 3.3). This is the best CFU figure found. It is a review-level statement, so check the primary data.
- **Level:** A (with author/year gaps) | **Volatility:** low
- **Link:** "Fermentation Molds" (ID 2966) for contrast and spoilage context (surface molds/yeasts); "Best Probiotic Foods" (ID 3342).

### 2.2 Salt concentration
- **Fact:** Final salinity of finished kimchi is typically about 2-3%, not 2-5%. Examples: a 2002 Korean study brined napa cabbage in 5-15% NaCl, and the cabbage brined at 15% ended at about 2.49% salt and scored well sensorily. A 1995 study found salt in kimchi solids stayed within about 2.4-3.0% throughout fermentation.
- **Fact:** Brine used in salting measured about 12-16% in Chinese-cabbage salting studies (Korean Journal of Food Science and Technology, koreascience.kr JAKO200011920158487).
- **Fact:** Low-salt kimchi is also made. A 1997 study on white (non-chili) kimchi tested 0.5-3.0% and rated 1.0% most acceptable.
- **Fact (effect on fermentation):** Evidence is mixed. One study across 1.0-2.1% salt found pH, acidity, viable counts and coliform counts were not affected. Others report that higher temperature plus lower salt ferments faster than lower temperature plus higher salt. Salt and temperature both shape microbial succession in reviews.
- **Fact (role):** Salt draws water out of the vegetable by osmosis, favors salt-tolerant LAB and holds back unwanted bacteria while LAB establish themselves. Organic acids then lower pH and inhibit acid-intolerant microbes.
- **Source for role:** microbial-ecology review material surfaced in search (PMC8465840, *Foods* 2021, "foods-10-02148"; exact title [확인 필요]).
- **No formal standard** for kimchi salinity was found. The 2-3% range reflects common practice in studies.
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low
- **Link:** "Salt and Fermentation" (ID 2960).

### 2.3 pH and acidity
- **Fact:** pH falls and titratable acidity rises as LAB produce acids.
- **Data points:**
  - One Korean temperature study: pH fell from 6.17 to 3.48-3.92 depending on temperature, with titratable acidity rising to about 1.1-1.5%. Exact citation [확인 필요].
  - A 2019 pasteurization study (PMC6392831): pH fell from 6.39 to 4.34 and acidity rose from 0.06% to 0.35% over 4 weeks at 4°C.
  - Optimal ripening is described around pH 4.1-4.2 (one Korean source), and 4.0-4.5 in a 2025 napa kimchi study, reached in about 3 days at 15°C.
- **Brief vs evidence:** The brief says pH starts at about 5.5-6 and drops to about 4.0-4.5. Studies found starting pH nearer 6.2-6.4 for fresh seasoned cabbage. "About 6" at the start and "about 4.0-4.5 at optimum ripeness, lower if over-fermented" is supported. Do not publish a precise start value without picking one study.
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low
- **Note:** Titratable acidity values differ by method (as % lactic acid), so avoid quoting a single "ideal acidity." [확인 필요]

### 2.4 Temperature
- **Fact:** Lower temperature slows fermentation. At 4°C, cut-cabbage kimchi reached pH 4.0 after 42 days at 0°C and 18 days at 4°C; marketable shelf-life was set at 42, 19 and 6 days at 0, 4 and 8°C (Korean study, 1995, koreascience.kr JAKO199511919821910; one product type only).
- **Fact:** A 2025 study (AGRIS record 68caad03f61e5b80b5e8fb13) held napa kimchi at 4°C or 15°C. At 15°C the optimal pH range (4.0-4.5) was reached within 3 days. Prolonged storage at 4°C into the excessive-fermentation stage reduced total phenolics, radical-scavenging activity and LAB counts.
- **Fact:** Higher temperature raised mannitol production (acta pol. 2025 paper, food.actapol.net/pub/2_2_2025.pdf). Temperature and salt alter succession.
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low

### 2.5 Metabolites
- **Fact:** Heterofermentative *Leuconostoc* produce lactate, acetate, ethanol, carbon dioxide and mannitol. Homofermentative *Lactobacillus* mainly produce lactic acid. This is why kimchi turns more sour in mid-to-late fermentation (Springer, *npj Science of Food* 2025, s41538-025-00415-w; and *Foods* 2021 review).
- **Fact:** *Leuc. mesenteroides* is described as a mostly dextran-producing heterofermentative LAB (PMC5599536).
- **Fact:** Lactic and acetic acid, mannitol, free sugars and amino acids are key flavor contributors (*Applied Biological Chemistry* 2025, s13765-025-01025-6). Lactic acid is the characteristic organic acid.
- **Starter culture detail:** High-rate *Leuc. mesenteroides* inoculation yielded more mannitol and acetic acid; *L. sakei* favored lactic acid (AGRIS 65dfd9180f3e94b9e5dcd4de).
- **CO2:** Responsible for the bubbles, the fizz, and swollen containers. Container study in 6.1 below.
- **Gap:** Nothing found on dextran's contribution to flavor or texture, only that it is produced. State only that *Leuc. mesenteroides* is a known dextran producer.
- **Level:** A/B | **Volatility:** low

## 3. Nutrition and health

**Writing rules:** Hedge every health statement ("studies suggest", "in small trials", "in animals"). Do not use "cures", "treats", "prevents". Include a disclaimer (draft in 3.5).

### 3.1 Nutrient profile
- **Fact (USDA, "Cabbage, kimchi," FDC ID 170392, per 100 g):** about 15 kcal, 1.1 g protein, 0.5 g fat, 2.4 g carbohydrate, 1.6 g fiber, folate (DFE) 52 µg, vitamin K 43.6 µg. Seen only through a mirror (getfoodfacts.com), which had an internal inconsistency over serving basis (100 g vs 150 g). **Must be confirmed on fdc.nal.usda.gov before use.** [확인 필요]
- **Fact:** Vitamin C: a 1989 Korean study found total vitamin C of about 19.8-24.7 mg/100 g at the start of fermentation, often dipping during fermentation. Later studies show ascorbic acid generally decreases over the fermentation period and varies with temperature and cabbage variety. Level B (old). No modern authoritative vitamin C value was found.
- **Fact:** A 1994 review describes kimchi as a useful source of vitamins, minerals and dietary fiber, with ascorbic acid and sugars changing during fermentation.
- **Gaps:** Vitamin A (carotene), B vitamins, B12 and mineral values were NOT confirmed. Do not claim "rich in vitamin A/B12." Raw cabbage and radish contribute fiber and some vitamins; fermentation changes some of them. [확인 필요]
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low (but product variation is large; specify "varies by recipe")

### 3.2 Gut and microbiome evidence (human)
- **Fact:** Wastyk et al., "Gut-microbiota-targeted diets modulate human immune status," *Cell*, July 2021 (DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2021.06.019). 36 healthy adults were randomized for 10 weeks to a high-fiber diet or a diet rich in fermented foods. The fermented-food group showed increased microbiome diversity and lower levels of 19 inflammatory markers. Participants chose their own fermented foods (including kimchi, yogurt, kefir, kombucha). **The study did not isolate kimchi's effect.** Small, short, proof-of-concept. (One repository listed 17 weeks, but most coverage and Stanford report 10 weeks: use 10.) Source also: https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation
- **Fact:** Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) trial: *Food & Nutrition Research* 66, May 2022, "Kimchi improves irritable bowel syndrome: results of a randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled study" (DOI 10.29219/fnr.v66.8268; PMC9180131). 90 adults (17 men, 73 women) in three groups of 30, each eating 210 g/day of kimchi for 12 weeks (standard kimchi, standard kimchi plus dead nano-sized *L. plantarum* nF1, or "functional" kimchi). IBS symptoms, defecation time and stool type improved; serum TNF-α fell in all groups; fecal β-glucosidase and β-glucuronidase fell; Firmicutes rose at the expense of Bacteroidetes and Tenericutes. Authors concluded kimchi alleviated IBS via fiber and reduced inflammatory cytokines.
  - **Caution:** All arms ate kimchi (no inert control), so the "placebo-controlled" label deserves a check against the full text; frame as "symptoms improved in all groups eating kimchi." One trial. Authors [확인 필요].
- **Fact:** A 12-week three-arm trial of overweight adults (placebo vs naturally fermented kimchi powder vs starter-culture kimchi powder) reported effects on helper T-cell function (news reports; participant numbers conflict between reports: 38 vs 13). Another study concluded short-term kimchi consumption had no immunomodulatory effect. **Immune evidence is mixed; do not use the participant counts.** [확인 필요]
- **Fact:** Scoping review of kimchi RCTs: *Journal of Ethnic Foods* (2023; s42779-023-00173-8). Lists RCTs in healthy subjects and others (e.g., Choi et al. 2013, n=100, 7 days). Outcomes include serum lipids, gut microbiota, iron status, obesity and metabolic parameters. A consumer-news blog claimed "11 trials, 608 participants" - **unverified, do not use**. Overall evidence: small, short, heterogeneous.
- **Level:** A for existence of studies; B for details | **Volatility:** medium (new trials appear; a UC Davis RCT, NCT07435831, on fermented vs unfermented cabbage and gut microbiome, and a kimchi-powder gut-health trial, NCT07749092, are listed. Recruiting status is time-sensitive: re-check or omit.)

### 3.3 Probiotic status
- **Fact:** Cha et al., "Does kimchi deserve the status of a probiotic food?" *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition* 64(19):6512-6525 (2024; online 2023; DOI 10.1080/10408398.2023.2170319). Surveys kimchi's health functions, the functions of kimchi LAB, and effects on the gut. LAB in kimchi reach about 9-10 log CFU/g. I could not see the review's conclusion. [확인 필요]
- **Fact:** Strain-level evidence is mostly in vitro. Example: *L. plantarum* C182 from kimchi showed resistance to low pH and 0.3% bile salts in vitro (2016 report in FoodNavigator/NutraIngredients). Authors said animal tests were still needed.
- **Fact:** Park et al. 2014 (see 1.1) lists proposed functions (anticancer, anti-obesity, cholesterol-lowering, antioxidant, immune-promoting), mostly from lab, animal and small human studies.
- **What the article can say:** Kimchi contains abundant live LAB; some strains survive acid and bile in lab tests; whether kimchi meets the formal definition of a "probiotic food" (specific strain, adequate dose, demonstrated benefit) is debated and the evidence in humans is limited. Specific strain survival counts in human guts: **not found**. [확인 필요]
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** medium
- **Link:** "Best Probiotic Foods" (ID 3342); "Probiotics vs. Postbiotics" (ID 3525).

### 3.4 Sodium and risk context
- **Fact:** Reported kimchi sodium varies widely. One Korean study estimated about 830 mg per 100 g (IFT *Food Technology*, "Kimchi: Salt and Spice and Everything Nice, or Not?"; looks high, base data not checked). Other values: 379.8 and 553.0 mg/100 g for two retail products (Consumers Korea test), and a Seoul study range of 46.9-468.6 mg/100 g. A consumer-page "500 mg per serving" has no source (C).
- **Fact:** Kimchi is cited as contributing 16-28% of daily sodium intake in Koreans (stated in a rat-study paper; secondary). [확인 필요]
- **Fact:** The WHO sodium guideline is under 2,000 mg/day (about 5 g salt). The IFT article's arithmetic on kimchi intake vs that limit is an estimate, not a measured intake. WHO figure itself not re-opened in this session [확인 필요].
- **Fact:** High salt intake is associated with gastric cancer in a meta-analysis of prospective studies (Habitual salt intake and risk of gastric cancer, PMID 22296873: high vs low intake RR about 1.68). A 2020 meta-analysis (PMC7225928) found each 40 g/day increase in pickled vegetables raised gastric cancer risk by 15%. For kimchi itself, the link is not established: some case-control studies suggest higher risk at very high intake, and others find lower risk with moderate intake; recall bias, H. pylori and confounding are cited. Recommended framing: "Kimchi is a salty food; people watching sodium (for example blood pressure) may want to consider portion size and low-sodium recipes."
- **Gap:** No kimchi-specific hypertension meta-analysis found.
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** low-medium
- **Link:** "Salt and Fermentation" (ID 2960).

## 6. Storage and fermentation conditions

### 6.1 Onggi vs modern containers
- **Fact:** Kim and Hu, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 2023 (rsif20230034): salted napa cabbage fermented in onggi versus sealed glass. CO2 in onggi was less than half that in glass. Authors attribute this to gas escaping through the walls, with LAB doing better; in sealed glass, LAB were hampered by their own CO2. Compared with glass, not plastic. Press: Georgia Tech via ScienceDaily, 2023-04-12 (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/04/230412153126.htm). Sample sizes appear small. Author names are from memory [확인 필요].
- **Fact:** Jeong et al. (2011; journal [확인 필요]): kimchi fermented at 4°C for four weeks in onggi, polyethylene, polypropylene, stainless steel and glass. Onggi showed higher gas permeability and better sensory/functional properties. A cited claim of "100 times higher LAB counts" is **unconfirmed**.
- **Level:** A/B | **Volatility:** low

### 6.2 Kimchi refrigerator
- **Fact:** The modern kimchi refrigerator market is usually dated to 1995 (Winia/Mando "Dimchae"; Samsung "Damat"). LG (as Goldstar) is said to have sold a 45-litre unit in 1984 (Korea JoongAng Daily caption), so "first" is contested. Do not say "first."
- **Fact:** The design goal is a narrow, stable temperature around -1°C (Samsung executive quoted; modern units also have ripening modes). Traditionally earthenware crocks were buried about 30 cm underground. A Winia Dimchae product planner said technology shortened the one year needed for "ripened kimchi" to six weeks (Korea Times, per search).
- **Why dedicated fridges:** Kimchi kept in a regular fridge could over-ferment after about a month; odor; space during kimjang; cold-chain temperature swings. A door open 30 minutes changes a kimchi fridge by only 2-5°C (retailer/press claim, B/C).
- **Unverified:** "over 98% of Korean homes have one" is from a KOCIS webzine and was not independently confirmed [확인 필요]. First-year sales figures (about 5,000 units in 1995; 530,000 in 1999) are from an aggregator citing Korea Times (C).
- **Level:** B | **Volatility:** medium

### 6.3 Optimal temperature and shelf life
- **Fact:** See 2.4. Practical guide supported by studies: refrigerator temperatures (0-4°C) slow ripening to weeks (about 18-42 days to reach pH 4.0 in one cut-cabbage study), while about 15°C gets to optimal pH in about 3 days.
- **Not found:** An authoritative definition of "fresh / ripe / over-ripe" with day counts, or "kimchi lasts X months." One consumer page claimed more than a year in the cold: **unsupported, do not use.** Suggested wording: "ripening time depends on temperature, salt and recipe; strongly sour aged kimchi (mukeunji) is valued for cooking." Any "weeks to months in the fridge" statement needs a source [확인 필요].
- **Link:** "Mukeunji / Aged Kimchi" (ID 2908); "How to Store Fermented Foods" (ID 2848).

## 7. FAQ data points

| Question | Finding | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Is kimchi vegan? | Traditional recipes use jeotgal (salted seafood) and/or fish sauce, so most traditional kimchi is not vegan. Vegan versions use miso, soup soy sauce, seaweed (kombu/wakame) or mushroom powder. A Brown University item reports kimchi made without fish products has the same types of bacteria as traditional kimchi. Check labels, as some store brands are labelled vegan. | B |
| Is kimchi gluten-free? | **Not confirmed.** Search returned nothing on wheat-flour paste or gochugaru. Traditional paste is usually rice-based, but some recipes/commercial products may use wheat flour or soy sauce. Write "often, but check labels." [확인 필요] | - |
| Does cooking kill probiotics? | Yes, live bacteria do not survive cooking heat; this is stated in the *Journal of Ethnic Foods* 2024 paper (s42779-024-00232-8, "Immunoenhancing and antioxidant potentials of kimchi... as a probiotic and postbiotic food"). Steaming killed microbes but did not change antioxidant activity; in mice both raw and cooked kimchi raised splenic lymphocytes and intestinal IgA. Animal data only. Lactic and acetic acid are heat-stable. | A (animal) |
| What is the kill temperature? | The brief's 115°F/46°C is **not supported**. It traces to a supplement-company blog. Lab data for lactobacilli show strain-dependent limits: no change at 54°C for 5 min; about 60-63°C a critical point; about 5-log loss after 60°C for 30 min (free cells). Some *L. plantarum* strains begin dying around 50-56°C (wiki, C). Use: "heat above roughly 50-60°C kills most live lactic acid bacteria, depending on strain and time; cooking temperatures are well above this." Verify with a primary food-microbiology source. [확인 필요] | B/C |

Daily kimchi intake and portion size fell from 1998 to 2012. Annual: Ministry of Agriculture (via KOCIS) 37 kg/person/year, survey year not stated; 36.1 kg in 2016 (press, 2016 data); 29 kg in 1999 (KREI). Aggregator sites with 14-53 kg are unreliable. **No official recent national figure confirmed.** Use "roughly 100 g per day, or 35-40 kg per year, in recent decades, and declining" with the caveat. ## 9. Change detection / conflicts with the brief's assumptions

1. **Salt "2-5%"**: Studies found final salinity of about 2-3% (2.49% at 15% brine; 2.4-3.0% in a 1995 study), with low-salt kimchi at 0.5-3.0% in research. Brine strength 12-16% is a different number. Suggest "about 2-3% in the finished kimchi; the cabbage is typically salted in a much stronger brine, roughly 12-16% in studies."
2. **pH "starts about 5.5-6"**: Measured start values were 6.17 and 6.39, so "about 6" is better. End value 4.0-4.5 is supported (optimal); over-ripe can go to 3.5-3.9.
3. **"115°F/46°C"**: Not supported by a primary source. See FAQ.
4. **Health-study citations I guessed in my own search queries were partly wrong**: a "Kim 2011" prediabetes trial is actually a 2013 RCT of fresh vs fermented kimchi in 21 prediabetic adults (PubMed 23969321). In it, improved glucose tolerance occurred in 9.5% (fresh) vs 33.3% (fermented), a small, exploratory result: use only with heavy hedging. A "Kim 2011" overweight/obese trial was published in *Nutrition Research* 31(6):436-443, 2011, "Fermented kimchi reduces body weight and improves metabolic parameters in overweight and obese patients." A "Han 2015" obese-women trial appeared in *Molecular Nutrition & Food Research* (24 women, fresh vs fermented kimchi, 8 weeks), not *Nutrition Research and Practice*. Authors, outcomes and effect sizes for these are [확인 필요] from full texts.
5. **Functional ingredient claims in the brief (capsaicin antimicrobial, garlic/ginger antimicrobial, jeotgal "additional microbes", rice paste as LAB carbohydrate)**: not confirmed in this research. Garlic as the main LAB source IS confirmed. Reword or find sources.
6. **"Leuconostoc early, Lactobacillus later"**: broadly supported, but species vary (see 2.1).
7. **"100-300+ types"**: Documented counts found are "200+" and "about 300." No support for a figure as low as 100.
